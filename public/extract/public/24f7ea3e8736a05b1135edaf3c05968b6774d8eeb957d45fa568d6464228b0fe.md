# AARO Workshop Tackled How to Collect and Analyze UAP Reports

**AARO**

> In August 2025, the Pentagon's All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office (AARO) sponsored a two-day workshop in Washington, DC, bringing together 40 participants from government, academia, and independent research to address problems with collecting, organizing, and analyzing UAP reports. The workshop focused entirely on data methodology — not on what UAPs are — and concluded that most reports are fragmented and unstructured, that AI tools carry serious risks of bias, and that classification and stigma remain major barriers to better data.

On August 5–6, 2025, the All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office (AARO) — the Pentagon office responsible for investigating unidentified anomalous phenomena — sponsored a workshop in the Washington, DC area. Hosted by Associated Universities, Inc. (AUI) and organized in collaboration with Florida State University, the event gathered 40 participants from government agencies, universities, and independent research groups. It built on a previous National Science Foundation–funded workshop held in 2024.

The workshop was not about determining what UAPs are or where they come from. Instead, it focused on a practical problem: the data behind UAP reports is a mess. Participants discussed how to handle military logs, pilot reports, civilian testimony, social media posts, radar data, weather records, and more. They found that most UAP reports are fragmented, sparse, and unstructured, making large-scale analysis difficult. Three breakout sessions covered how to identify and integrate data sources, how to analyze data at scale, and how to clean and organize it.

Several major obstacles were identified. Classification remains a dominant barrier — much UAP data may sit on classified sensors, making it inaccessible. Stigma around reporting, particularly among pilots, means incidents go unreported or are reported late. Weak data-retention policies have led to the loss of critical records, with the well-known 2004 Nimitz incident cited as an example. Access to social media data has also tightened due to corporate licensing restrictions.

The workshop raised pointed concerns about artificial intelligence. Participants noted that large language models are already biased by UFO-related cultural content in their training data, which could skew any AI-assisted analysis. AI-generated photos and videos pose risks to both public trust and analytic integrity. Crowdsourced transcription projects were found to suffer from low-quality outputs made worse by misuse of generative AI. Recommendations included developing standardized metadata templates, creating report triage systems, adopting cautious hybrid human-AI approaches, and improving public reporting portals.

The workshop operated under Institutional Review Board approval governing its data collection and security. Participant statements were anonymized throughout the published report. The event was not publicized beforehand, and attendance was limited to invited experts.

## What to know

- This document is a workshop discussion synthesis, not official government policy or a statement of findings about UAP themselves. It reflects what participants discussed, not what the government has concluded.
- No specific UAP incidents or sightings are analyzed in this report — it is purely about data methodology. The Nimitz case is mentioned only as an example of lost records.
- Participant statements were anonymized, so individual claims cannot be attributed or independently verified against a named source.
- The document includes both consensus views and individual suggestions without always distinguishing between them, so not every recommendation necessarily had full agreement.
- The workshop was invitation-only and not publicly announced beforehand, meaning the participant pool was curated rather than open.
