# Commercial Pilot Reports Bright White Lights Near Chicago O'Hare

**FAA · 2023**

> An FAA SKYWATCH incident report documents a commercial pilot on Royal Jordanian flight RJA264 observing bright white lights approximately 25 nautical miles north of Chicago O'Hare International Airport on 2 March 2023. The case was closed with no identification of the phenomenon and no law enforcement was notified.

This document is a standardised FAA SKYWATCH incident report — a form the Federal Aviation Administration uses to log pilot sightings of unidentified aerial phenomena (UAP). It records what the pilot reported, not what was independently verified.

On 2 March 2023, the crew of Royal Jordanian flight RJA264, a commercial flight with a destination listed as OJAI, reported an unidentified aerial phenomenon while flying northbound at flight level 180 (approximately 18,000 feet). The lights were observed off the left side of the aircraft, roughly 25 nautical miles north of Chicago O'Hare International Airport (ORD). The crew described them as bright white lights, situated between 14,000 feet and FL180, and explicitly stated that the phenomenon was not a drone or unmanned aircraft system (UAS).

The report is filed under MOR ID ZAU-M-2023/03/01-0002, referencing the Chicago Air Route Traffic Control Center (ZAU). No law enforcement agency was notified about the sighting.

The FAA marked the case as closed. No resolution or identification of the phenomenon is provided in the document. There is no indication that radar data, additional witness accounts, or follow-up investigation results were included.

## What to know

- This is a single-source pilot report. No corroborating radar data or additional witness statements are included in the document.
- The FAA SKYWATCH form captures what was reported by the crew — it does not represent an FAA determination about what the lights were.
- The case is marked closed with no explanation or identification of the phenomenon, which is standard for many FAA UAP filings.
- Redaction levels appear low, but the absence of follow-up findings means the document is limited in what it can tell us beyond the initial sighting report.
- Some details, such as the point of departure listed as DEN (Denver), carry slightly lower confidence in the verified fact set.
