# Two Commercial Flights Reported Unexplained Lights Over Colorado in 2024

**FAA · 2023**

> This FAA SKYWATCH report documents an incident on 25 January 2024 in which an Alaska Airlines crew flying at 38,000 feet observed three white lights flashing on and off directly ahead of and above their aircraft. A second crew on a SkyWest flight at 32,000 feet independently reported the same phenomenon, but nothing appeared on air traffic control radar. The case was closed with no identification or explanation provided.

On 25 January 2024 at 04:47 UTC, the crew of Alaska Airlines flight ASA440 — a Boeing 737-800 flying westbound from Miami to Portland at 38,000 feet — reported an unidentified aerial phenomenon to Denver Air Route Traffic Control Centre (ZDV). The crew described three white lights turning on and off consistently, positioned directly in front of and well above the aircraft, approximately 26 nautical miles east of the CKW navigation point in Colorado.

The sighting was not a single-crew observation. A SkyWest Airlines flight, SKW5152, operating a CRJ-200 at 32,000 feet, independently reported observing the same phenomenon. Two separate commercial aircraft crews seeing the same thing from different altitudes adds weight to the observation, though both accounts are still visual reports from cockpit crews rather than instrument readings.

Air traffic control checked their radar systems and found nothing. The FAA report states plainly: the phenomenon was not observed on the ATC facility radar system. Whatever the crews saw left no radar signature.

The FAA classified the report under its primary code of "Unidentified Aerial Phenomenon" and marked the case as closed. No follow-up investigation is documented in the file, and no explanation or identification was offered. The document shows low evidence of redaction — what you see appears to be substantially what was recorded.

## What to know

- This is a single incident report with no documented follow-up investigation — the FAA closed the case without resolving what the lights were.
- Two independent flight crews corroborate the sighting, which is stronger than a single-witness report, but both observations are visual only — no radar or instrument data supports them.
- Descriptions like 'well above' and 'directly in front' are subjective pilot impressions, not precise measurements of distance or altitude.
- The absence of a radar return does not confirm or rule out any particular explanation — some objects and atmospheric phenomena do not produce radar signatures.
- The document shows low evidence of redaction, meaning it appears to be a straightforward, largely complete incident report rather than a heavily censored file.
