# Pentagon-Backed Study Links UAP Sightings to Air Traffic and Visibility

**OSD · 2023**

> This 2023 academic paper, co-authored by the then-director of the Pentagon's UAP office, analysed nearly 99,000 public UFO sighting reports across the US from 2001 to 2020. It found that sighting rates rise where there is more air traffic and military activity, and fall where light pollution or tree cover limits sky visibility — suggesting most reports reflect people seeing conventional objects in unexpected ways.

This document is a draft academic paper cleared for open publication on 17 February 2023 by the Office of the Secretary of Defense (OSD). It was proposed for submission to the Annals of the American Association of Geographers. The authors are Richard M. Medina and Simon Brewer of the University of Utah, alongside Sean M. Kirkpatrick, who at the time led the All-Domain Anomaly Resolution Office (AARO) — the Pentagon's dedicated UAP investigation unit, established on 20 July 2022.

The study drew on 122,983 sighting reports from the National UFO Reporting Center (NUFORC), a public database founded in 1974. Focusing on 98,724 sightings in the contiguous United States between 2001 and 2020, the researchers used Bayesian statistical modelling to test whether sighting rates correlate with environmental factors such as light pollution, cloud cover, tree canopy, nearby airports, and military installations.

The results showed clear patterns. Counties with more air traffic reported more sightings, with 100% statistical credibility. Areas near military installations also showed higher rates, at 92% credibility. Conversely, heavier light pollution reduced sightings by about 7.7% per standard-deviation increase, and denser tree canopy was also linked to fewer reports. Cloud cover showed no credible relationship in either direction. Sighting hotspots clustered in the western US, the far northeast, the Illinois-Indiana-Kentucky border region, and around Washington DC. Reports peaked nationally between 2012 and 2014, then dropped sharply through 2018.

The authors concluded that the data support a straightforward explanation: people report UAP when they have more opportunity to look at the sky and more things flying through it. They noted that the strong link to air traffic and military activity suggests observers are seeing human-made objects in unexpected ways. The paper does not claim to explain every sighting, nor does it rule out genuinely anomalous cases — it addresses statistical patterns across a large dataset, not individual incidents.

## What to know

- The underlying data comes from NUFORC, a crowd-sourced public database. Reports are self-submitted and unverified — some may be hoaxes or misidentifications of known objects like satellites, drones, or weather balloons.
- Co-author Sean Kirkpatrick was then-director of AARO, the Pentagon's UAP office. The paper may reflect an institutional perspective that most sightings have conventional explanations.
- The study analyses county-level statistical patterns, not individual sighting cases. It does not attempt to explain what any specific witness saw.
- Cloud cover results were inconclusive, possibly because competing regional effects (the cloudy Pacific Northwest and the clear Mountain West both have high sighting rates) cancelled each other out at the national scale.
- Natural phenomena such as ball lightning or seismic luminosity were not tested as variables because recorded instances are too rare to model statistically.
