FBI Investigated Flying Saucer Lecturers, Contactees, and Civilian UFO Groups in 1954

FBI ✓ verified against source

This section of FBI case file 62-HQ-83894 contains 1954 investigative memoranda about a paid flying saucer lecture tour by Truman Bethurum, a Maine woman named Frances Swan who claimed telepathic contact with beings from outer space, and civilian UFO organizations including the Detroit Flying Saucer Club and Leonard Stringfield's CRIFO newsletter. No agency — FBI, Navy, or Air Force — took substantive action on any of the claims.

This batch of FBI documents from 1954 covers the Bureau's interest in three threads of civilian flying saucer activity. The first involves Truman Bethurum, a truck driver from Redondo Beach, California, who claimed he had been aboard flying saucers on eleven occasions and encountered a female commandant in the Nevada desert. He toured the lecture circuit charging $2.00 per ticket; one planned event at the Taft Auditorium in Cincinnati was projected to draw 2,000 people and gross $4,000. The FBI's Cincinnati field office opened an inquiry — filed under an espionage classification — into whether the lectures constituted fraud. The second thread concerns Frances Swan of South Berwick, Maine, who said she had been receiving telepathic messages from beings in outer space since May 27, 1954. She identified two supposed commanders: "AFFA," managing a craft called M-4 from the planet Uranus, and "PONNAR," commanding L-11 from a planet called Hatann. A Navy security officer named Hutson visited Swan at the home of retired Admiral Herbert B. Knowles, who took her claims seriously enough to write letters about them to Senator Margaret Chase Smith, the Secretary of Defense, and the President. Canadian government physicist Wilber B. Smith also visited in an unofficial capacity and planned to attempt radio contact with outer space from near Ottawa on August 1, 1954. Despite all this activity, the Office of Naval Intelligence (ONI) declined to act, citing the absence of definite evidence, and the FBI forwarded the matter to Air Force investigators before closing its own involvement. The third thread tracks civilian UFO organizations. Leonard H. Stringfield ran the Civilian Research, Interplanetary Flying Objects (CRIFO) organization in Cincinnati, publishing a newsletter with a claimed worldwide circulation of about 4,000 copies at $2.00 per year. A coworker named A. Scott Gunderman reported Stringfield's activities to the FBI, speculating that the group might be gathering information about a secret Air Force project. Separately, the Detroit Flying Saucer Club sent a petition to President Eisenhower requesting disclosure of flying saucer information, and a group of amateur radio operators within the club claimed to have made contact with a saucer. An Air Force officer, Lt. Col. O'Mara, gave contradictory statements to different people — telling one man that flying saucers did not exist and that author Donald Kehoe was a fraud, while reportedly telling Stringfield that flying saucers do exist and that past contradictions were unfortunate. FBI Director J. Edgar Hoover's office handled several pieces of correspondence during this period, including a letter stating that a MYSTIC magazine article about the FBI was entirely incorrect. Across the board, the Bureau monitored civilian flying saucer enthusiasm but took no further investigative action on the claims themselves.

What to know

  • Frances Swan's telepathic contact claims were reported testimony only — no agency (FBI, ONI, or Navy) verified or endorsed them, and all declined further investigation.
  • Lt. Col. O'Mara's contradictory statements about whether flying saucers exist are documented but never explained in the file, making it impossible to know which statement reflected his actual view or Air Force policy.
  • The coworker who reported Stringfield's CRIFO organization to the FBI had possible personal motivations; his suspicion that the group served espionage purposes was his own speculation, not an FBI finding.
  • Admiral Knowles and Canadian physicist Wilber B. Smith were acting in unofficial, personal capacities — their involvement should not be read as endorsement by the U.S. Navy or the Canadian government.
  • Many pages in this section are heavily degraded and partially unreadable, meaning some information may be missing from the record.
