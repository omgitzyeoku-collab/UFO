FBI Tracked 150 Unexplained Green Fireballs Near New Mexico Military Sites

FBI ✓ verified against source

This FBI case file documents roughly 150 sightings of unusual aerial objects — green fireballs, discs, and meteors — near sensitive military installations in New Mexico between late 1948 and 1950. Scientists could not explain the green fireballs, the Air Force launched a formal study called Project Twinkle, and the official conclusion was that the objects were not spacecraft or foreign missiles, though no positive identification was ever made.

This section of an FBI case file collects internal memos, letters, and a scientific summary dealing with unexplained aerial phenomena spotted near US military and government sites in New Mexico from December 1948 through 1950. The phenomena were sorted into three categories: green fireballs, discs, and meteors. Approximately 150 observations were logged, with 72 objects specifically classified as green fireballs. Dr. Lincoln LaPaz of the University of New Mexico led the scientific analysis. He identified at least 10 characteristics that set green fireballs apart from ordinary meteors, including horizontal flight paths, low altitude, no audible sound, and a distinctive green colour that laboratory tests matched to copper salts at a wavelength near 5218 Angstroms. LaPaz believed the non-meteoric objects were most likely US guided missiles being tested near the installations. If they were not American, he suggested they could theoretically be missiles launched from the Ural region of the USSR, capable of reaching New Mexico in under 15 minutes. Dr. Joseph Kaplan, a US Air Force scientific adviser, and aerodynamicist Theodore Von Karman both stated they knew of no US experiments that would account for the objects. Two conferences were held at Los Alamos in February and October 1949, attended by representatives from the FBI, the US Atomic Energy Commission, the Fourth Army, and other agencies. No explanation for the green fireballs was reached, but attendees agreed the phenomena were real and warranted formal scientific study. The Air Force then contracted Land-Air, Inc. of Alamogordo, New Mexico, to run Project Twinkle — a 24-hour observation watch with posts near Vaughn, New Mexico. On 24 May 1950, Land-Air personnel sighted 8 to 10 aerial objects. The file also covers a separate incident on 26 September 1950, when two Philadelphia police officers reported a six-foot-diameter lavender glowing mass that disintegrated within 25 minutes, leaving a sticky residue. No physical evidence was preserved. Additionally, the FBI was instructed to investigate whether Frank Scully, author of the book 'Behind the Flying Saucers', had ties to Communist activities. By October 1950, the Air Force Office of Special Investigations (OSI) at Wright Field, Ohio, concluded that their investigation 'fails to indicate that the sightings involved space ships or missiles from any other planet or country' — a negative finding that ruled things out without identifying what the objects actually were. The file shows green fireballs were mostly seen between 5 PM and 11 PM, peaking around 8:30 PM, with a notable concentration on weekends. Their flight paths showed a pronounced tendency to come from the north. Moderate redactions are present throughout the documents, and many pages are heavily degraded, with tabulated sighting data largely illegible.

What to know

  • The OSI conclusion was a negative finding — it said what the objects were NOT (not spacecraft, not foreign missiles) but never positively identified what they were.
  • Dr. LaPaz's preferred explanation (US guided missiles) was never confirmed. He himself noted that anyone in a position to confirm it would refuse to do so, hinting at possible classified programmes.
  • Many pages are heavily degraded and the tabulated sighting data is largely illegible due to poor document quality, meaning some details may be missing or misread.
  • The Philadelphia disintegrating-object incident was reported secondhand through police channels, and no physical evidence was preserved for analysis.
  • The document reflects jurisdictional tensions between the FBI and Air Force OSI, which may have affected what information was shared between agencies.
