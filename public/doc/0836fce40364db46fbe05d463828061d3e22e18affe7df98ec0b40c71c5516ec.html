Pentagon Office Explains How Satellite Reflections Cause UAP Reports

AARO ✓ verified against source

The All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office (AARO) published an informational paper in December 2024 explaining how sunlight reflecting off satellites — particularly SpaceX's Starlink constellation — produces bright flashes that are frequently reported as unidentified anomalous phenomena (UAP). The paper includes mathematical methods for predicting these flashes and resolves a specific pilot UAP report near Gallup, New Mexico as "very likely" satellite flares.

In December 2024, the Pentagon's All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office (AARO) released a paper explaining a common source of UAP reports: satellite flaring. Satellite flaring occurs when sunlight reflects off the surfaces of satellites in low Earth orbit (LEO), producing bright flashes visible from the ground or from aircraft. With over 6,700 Starlink satellites in orbit as of late November 2024 — and mega-constellations from Eutelsat OneWeb, Amazon Kuiper, and Chinese G60 also growing — these reflections are increasingly common. The paper describes how multiple satellites flaring at once can appear as spinning lights, glowing orbs that vanish and reappear, or even geometric shapes like triangles. This phenomenon is not new — it was well documented in the late 1990s and early 2000s with the Iridium satellite constellation — but the sheer number of satellites now in orbit has made it far more frequent. Starlink satellites operate at roughly 550 km altitude, and their flares are generally visible when the sun is between 38° and 46° below the horizon, appearing low on the sky up to about 10° above the horizon. AARO staff travelled to Sidney, Nebraska in March 2024 specifically to photograph Starlink flares for this paper. The paper also includes a worked case study: on October 9, 2022, a pilot flying at 35,000 feet near Gallup, New Mexico reported seeing "multiple lights moving in different directions, left of the constellation Leo." AARO's analysis concluded the lights were "very likely Starlink and other satellite flares." The paper also notes that pilots flying toward the sun after sunset or before sunrise can remain in the reflected light path longer, making airborne observers particularly prone to these sightings. This paper is educational in nature. It does not investigate unexplained cases or present evidence of unknown phenomena. Its purpose is to provide observers and analysts with the tools to identify satellite flares and distinguish them from genuinely anomalous sightings.

What to know

  • This is an explanatory paper, not an investigation of an unexplained event — AARO's goal here is to attribute certain UAP reports to a known cause.
  • Only one specific UAP case is analysed (the Gallup, NM pilot report), and the conclusion is "very likely" satellite flares, not a definitive confirmation.
  • The mathematical model described in the paper uses approximations and is described as accurate only to within a few degrees.
  • The paper has minimal redactions, and the document's overall evidence quality is rated high-confidence.
  • The paper represents AARO's prosaic-explanation perspective and does not address cases that remain unexplained.
