CIA Relayed 1955 Hungarian Letter Describing Fast-Moving Aerial Objects

CIA 1955 Hungary ± partially verified

A CIA information report passed along a secondhand account from a private letter written by a woman in Budapest, describing 'flying saucers' that had been observed over Hungary for several weeks in 1955 and were estimated to travel at 12,000 km/h. The CIA filed the report as unevaluated information with no independent verification.

In November 1955, a naturalised US citizen of Hungarian origin received a letter from his niece in Budapest. She described widespread public anxiety over fast-moving aerial objects — referred to as 'flying saucers' or 'rockets' — that had been seen over Hungary for several weeks. According to the letter, the objects were estimated to travel at 12,000 kilometres per hour, and scores of scientists were said to be actively studying the phenomena. The letter also included a sketch suggesting a flight path from Budapest toward Moscow. The CIA prepared and disseminated the report (number 00-B-93674) based entirely on this single piece of personal correspondence. The agency explicitly stamped it as 'unevaluated information' — meaning it had not been verified or analysed. A photostatic copy of the original Hungarian letter, along with an English translation, was held at the CIA Library. The report was classified for limited distribution only — restricted to full-time employees of the CIA, the Atomic Energy Commission (AEC), the FBI, and State and Defence intelligence components. No foreign dissemination was permitted. The document carries moderate redactions, which obscure the identities of the source, his niece, and other family members mentioned in the correspondence. This was the first time the niece had mentioned such objects in her letters, according to the source. The report offers no analysis, no corroboration from other intelligence channels, and no conclusion about what the objects were.

What to know

  • This is a single-source report based entirely on a private family letter — no independent corroboration was provided or cited by the CIA.
  • The CIA explicitly marked the report as 'unevaluated information', meaning the agency made no judgement about its accuracy or significance.
  • The letter writer was a civilian in Budapest, not a trained observer or scientist. The 12,000 km/h speed estimate has no stated measurement methodology and may be hearsay or drawn from press reports.
  • Moderate redactions obscure the identities of the people involved, limiting any independent verification.
  • Cold War context matters: reports from behind the Iron Curtain were inherently difficult to verify, and the suspected Budapest-to-Moscow flight path reflects the letter writer's personal speculation, not tracked radar data.
