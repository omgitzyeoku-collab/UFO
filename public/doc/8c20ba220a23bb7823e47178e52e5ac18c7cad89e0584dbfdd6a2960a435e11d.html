Commercial Pilot Reports White Cylindrical Object Near JFK Airport

FAA 2023 ✓ verified against source

An FAA SKYWATCH incident report documents a commercial aircraft encountering an unidentified white cylindrical object about 10 nautical miles south of JFK airport on 29 May 2023. The pilot classified it as a near miss at roughly 19,000 feet, but no evasive action was taken and the case was closed without apparent follow-up.

On the evening of 29 May 2023, at 22:20, the crew of commercial flight JRE785 reported an unidentified aerial phenomenon while flying northeast at Flight Level 190 — roughly 19,000 feet — about 10 nautical miles south of John F. Kennedy International Airport in New York. The pilot described the object as a white cylindrical shape, approximately the size of three bowling balls, travelling eastbound at roughly the same altitude as the aircraft. The pilot in command classified the encounter as a near miss, though no evasive manoeuvres were taken. The report was filed under the FAA's SKYWATCH system and coded as an Unidentified Aerial Phenomenon. The case status is marked as closed, with no indication in the document of any follow-up investigation, resolution, or identification of the object. The document is a single-page incident report with limited detail. It records only the pilot's account — there is no mention of corroborating radar data, additional witnesses, or any attempt to recover or further identify the object.

What to know

  • This is a single-source report based solely on the pilot's observation — no corroborating radar data or additional witnesses are mentioned in the document.
  • The object was never identified or recovered, so the pilot's description of its shape and size remains unverified.
  • The case is marked as closed with no indication of follow-up investigation or resolution by the FAA.
  • There is low evidence of redaction in this document — it appears to be a routine incident filing rather than a heavily censored record.
  • The term 'near miss' reflects the pilot's own classification, not an independent assessment of how close the object came to the aircraft.
