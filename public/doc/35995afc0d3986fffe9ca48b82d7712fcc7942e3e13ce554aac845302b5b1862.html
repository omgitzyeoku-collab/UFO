FBI File Compiled Press Clippings on UFO Sightings From 1954 to 1960

FBI ± partially verified

This is a portion of FBI case file 62-HQ-83894, a collection of newspaper clippings, wire service reports, and internal routing slips covering UFO sightings and investigations across the United States from roughly 1954 to 1960. The file documents accounts ranging from radar trackings and pilot encounters to a debunked contact claim, alongside official Air Force and expert responses to the sighting wave of the late 1950s.

This document is not an FBI investigation report. It is a compilation of newspaper clippings, wire service dispatches, and internal routing slips that the FBI gathered under case file 62-HQ-83894, covering UFO-related events from approximately 1954 to 1960. The bureau collected these materials but did not necessarily endorse or investigate the claims in them. The file covers a wide range of incidents. In November 1957, crew aboard the Coast Guard cutter Sebago tracked an unidentified object on radar for 27 minutes in the Gulf of Mexico, roughly 200 miles south of Louisiana, with four crew members also observing it visually. That same month, five people including a sheriff in Levelland, Texas reported a glowing object that stalled car engines and shut off headlights. A grain dealer named R. O. Schmidt claimed he encountered a landed spacecraft near Kearney, Nebraska, describing a translucent cigar-shaped craft about 100 feet long — but investigators found his story weakening under questioning, discovered he had a state penitentiary record, and noted that mysterious oil at the alleged landing site closely matched commercial auto oil from his car. The file also includes statements from prominent figures. Retired Rear Admiral Delmer Fahrney told the press that no agency in the United States or Russia could duplicate the reported speeds and accelerations of UFOs. Psychologist Carl Jung stated that UFOs were real and showed signs of intelligent guidance, and that a purely psychological explanation was ruled out. On the skeptical side, Harvard astronomer Donald Menzel attributed sightings to mirages caused by heated air, and General James Doolittle flatly denied claims that Nazi Germany had developed a flying saucer. An Air Force summary included in the file stated that its UFO program, established in 1947, had investigated 5,700 sightings through 1957, with over 80 percent explainable as balloons, aircraft, astronomical bodies, or similar known objects. The Air Force concluded it had received no evidence of observation by machines from outer space or a foreign government. Notable smaller details include a diamond-shaped object photographed near Amarillo, Texas by S. G. Kirby in August 1956, which was turned over to the FBI and released after study — the Air Force described the glow as "radiation vapor." Capital Airlines Captain W. J. Hull, who had previously written an article titled "The Obituary of the Flying Saucer," later reported his own encounter with an unidentified object near Mobile, Alabama. The Air Force also distributed special cameras with diffraction grating lenses to military installations starting in early 1953 to try to photograph the phenomena.

What to know

  • This file is a collection of press clippings and wire reports, not primary FBI investigative findings. The bureau collected these materials but did not necessarily verify or endorse the claims within them.
  • Many of the sighting reports are single-source eyewitness accounts without corroborating physical evidence. The Schmidt spacecraft encounter was actively undermined by investigators who found evidence of fabrication.
  • Statements from figures like Admiral Fahrney and Carl Jung were made in private or retired capacities — Fahrney as chairman of a private UFO committee, Jung through a private UFO research organization — not as official government or peer-reviewed positions.
  • The Air Force summary in the file represents its official institutional position and should be understood as such, not as independent analysis.
  • The original PDF has poor OCR quality in places, meaning some specific details drawn from the scanned text may be imprecise.
