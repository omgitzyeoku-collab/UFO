FAA Report: Pilot Saw Three Lights at 45,000 Feet Over Atlantic

FAA 2023 ✓ verified against source

This is an FAA SKYWATCH incident report documenting a general aviation pilot's sighting of an unidentified aerial phenomenon while flying from London Luton to Fort Lauderdale on 14 August 2022. The crew reported three lights in a row, with additional lights above them, observed off the left side of the aircraft at 45,000 feet roughly 195 nautical miles south of Wilmington, North Carolina. The case was closed with no explanation provided.

This document is an FAA SKYWATCH incident report — a standardised form the Federal Aviation Administration uses to log reports of unidentified aerial phenomena from pilots and air traffic controllers. It was filed under the identifier 237UAP00104 and classified with the primary code "Unidentified Aerial Phenomenon." On 14 August 2022 at 05:40, the crew of aircraft N728HG reported seeing something unusual while flying southwest-bound at flight level 450 (45,000 feet). The aircraft was approximately 195 nautical miles south of Wilmington, North Carolina (airport code ILM), on a general aviation flight from London Luton Airport (EGGW) in England to Fort Lauderdale (FLL) in Florida. The crew described seeing an unidentified aerial phenomenon off the left side of their aircraft. They reported it as three lights arranged in a row, with additional lights above them that they described as being in a "geosynchronous orbit." The sighting was reported through Jacksonville Center (ZJX), the FAA air traffic control facility covering that region, and a reference office designated AWO/TY was advised. The report's status is marked as closed. Notably, no resolution or explanation for the sighting is provided anywhere in the document. There is no indication that radar data, independent witness accounts, or any follow-up analysis was included or conducted. The FAA's processing office for this report was Denver (DEN).

What to know

  • This is a single pilot report with no corroborating radar data or independent witnesses mentioned in the document.
  • The crew's use of the term 'geosynchronous orbit' is their own description of what they saw — it is not a confirmed scientific determination of the object's trajectory or altitude.
  • The case is marked closed with no resolution, meaning the FAA did not publicly explain what the lights were.
  • No tail number or operator name beyond the callsign N728HG is listed, which limits independent verification of the flight.
  • The document references that AWO/TY was advised, but no follow-up report or analysis from that office is included.
