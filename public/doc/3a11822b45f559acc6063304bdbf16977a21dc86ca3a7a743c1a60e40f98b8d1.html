CIA History Details Secret U-2 and OXCART Spy Plane Programs

CIA 1954-1974 ✓ verified against source

This is a CIA internal history, written in 1992 and classified Secret, covering the development and operations of the U-2 and A-12 OXCART high-altitude reconnaissance aircraft from 1954 to 1974. It documents how Cold War intelligence needs drove radical aircraft design, how U-2 flights inadvertently generated UFO reports, and how the programmes reshaped the CIA itself.

This document is a CIA History Staff monograph written by Gregory W. Pedlow and Donald E. Welzenbach, published in April 1992 and classified Secret NOFORN (No Foreign Nationals). It chronicles 20 years of CIA-run aerial spying, from President Eisenhower's authorisation of the U-2 project in late 1954 through the transfer of the last CIA U-2s to the Air Force in 1974. The story begins in the early Cold War, when the Soviet Union grew increasingly aggressive about defending its airspace. On 8 April 1950, Soviet fighters shot down a US Navy Privateer patrol aircraft over the Baltic Sea — one of several incidents that underscored how dangerous conventional reconnaissance had become. The CIA needed an aircraft that could fly above Soviet defences. Clarence L. "Kelly" Johnson of Lockheed answered with the CL-282, essentially a jet-propelled glider: an F-104 fuselage fitted with sailplane wings, stressed to only 2.5 g's instead of the military standard 5.33 g's, capable of reaching just over 70,000 feet. The best Soviet interceptor of the era, the MIG-17, struggled to reach 45,000 feet. The aircraft became the U-2, codenamed Project AQUATONE. The U-2's first flight over the Soviet Union in July 1956 made it, according to the document, the most important single source of intelligence on the USSR. But Eisenhower remained reluctant about the flights, doubtful of their prudence. His caution proved warranted: on 1 May 1960, Soviet forces shot down pilot Francis Gary Powers, and Eisenhower banned further U-2 overflights of the USSR. The CIA then developed the faster A-12 OXCART as a successor, but it saw limited operational use. Its first deployment was Operation BLACK SHIELD. President Lyndon B. Johnson publicly revealed the OXCART-type aircraft at press conferences in February and July 1964, and the programme was cancelled in 1968 after the Air Force fielded its own variant, the SR-71. One notable detail: the document includes a chapter heading referencing U-2s, UFOs, and the Air Force's Operation BLUE BOOK — the connection being that U-2 flights at extreme altitude generated UFO sighting reports. However, the full chapter text explaining this link is not included in the extracted portion. The programmes' broader legacy was institutional: the CIA created its Directorate of Science and Technology in the 1960s principally to manage reconnaissance efforts like these.

What to know

  • This is a CIA internal history written by CIA staff using CIA records and selected interviews — it presents the Agency's own account of events, not an independent assessment.
  • The document carries moderate redaction: some names and organisational details are blacked out, and the authors note they deliberately kept certain satellite and interagency details vague due to the Secret classification level.
  • The UFO/BLUE BOOK connection appears only as a chapter heading in this extract — the full explanation of how U-2 flights generated UFO reports is not included in the text available here.
  • The monograph was published in 1992, decades after the events it describes, and relies partly on interviews with ageing participants whose recollections may be incomplete.
  • A more heavily redacted version of this document has previously been available on the CIA website; this release through the PURSUE programme may contain additional previously-redacted content.
