Pentagon UAP Office Briefing Explains Its Work and Three Case Studies

OSD 2023 ✓ verified against source

This February 2024 briefing from the Pentagon's All-Domain Anomaly Resolution Office (AARO) describes how the office is structured, what it has accomplished, and walks through three specific UAP cases — two of which were resolved as ordinary aircraft mistaken due to sensor quirks, and one (a metallic orb over the Middle East) that remains unexplained but showed nothing extraordinary.

In February 2024, the All-Domain Anomaly Resolution Office (AARO) — the Pentagon unit responsible for investigating unidentified anomalous phenomena (UAP) — prepared this unclassified mission briefing. The document was reviewed for public release on 5 February 2025 by the AARO Chief of Staff under authority granted by the FY24 National Defense Authorization Act. It lays out the office's four working areas: operations, science and technology, analysis, and public communications. The briefing is notable for how it defines UAP: detections across air, sea, space, or multiple domains that cannot yet be attributed to a known source and that behave in ways sensors or observers do not readily understand. It explicitly states that UAP are not the same thing as unidentified balloons. AARO frames the existence of UAP as a direct consequence of gaps in the military's ability to monitor its own airspace and surroundings. Three case studies are presented. In 2022, an MQ-9 drone observed what appeared to be a spherical, metallic object over the Middle East, consistent with other "metallic orb" sightings in the region. No unusual technical capabilities were observed, and the case remains in AARO's active archive without a firm explanation. The second case involved a P-3 patrol aircraft on a training mission over the western United States that spotted three objects spaced equally apart; air-traffic-control data showed they were likely commercial aircraft, and apparent shape changes turned out to be the sensor's autofocus adjusting. The third, from 2023 over South Asia, involved an MQ-9 observing an object that appeared to leave an atmospheric wake — analysis determined the object was a commercial airliner and the visible trail was caused by video-compression software overlaying one frame onto the next. Beyond the case studies, the briefing acknowledges significant data problems. A large number of AARO's cases remain technically unresolved not because the objects are mysterious, but because there simply is not enough data to reach a conclusion. Over 100 reports were received from the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA), but most consisted of "lights" sightings with no detail on shape or behaviour. AARO also notes that its reporting is biased toward military bases and testing ranges because that is where its sensors and personnel are concentrated — commercial-pilot reporting is helping to broaden that picture. Only a very small percentage of all UAP reports display genuinely anomalous signatures.

What to know

  • Two of the three case studies were resolved as mundane (commercial aircraft misidentified due to sensor or compression artifacts). The third (a metallic orb) is unresolved but showed no extraordinary capabilities. These cases may not be representative of AARO's full portfolio.
  • Many cases remain unresolved not because they are unexplainable, but because there is not enough data — most FAA reports, for example, were simple 'lights in the sky' sightings with no detail on the object's shape or behaviour.
  • Reporting is heavily biased toward military installations and test ranges. The geographic spread of UAP reports reflects where the military operates, not necessarily where UAP occur.
  • This is an institutional briefing prepared for departmental and congressional audiences. It reflects AARO's own perspective and may understate anomalous findings.
  • The briefing was last updated in February 2024 but was not cleared for release until February 2025, so there is a one-year gap between the content and its publication.
