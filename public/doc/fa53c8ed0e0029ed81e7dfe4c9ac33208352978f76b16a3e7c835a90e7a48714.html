Pentagon UAP Office Reports 757 Sightings, Finds No Alien Evidence

OSD 2023 ✓ verified against source

The Department of Defense's All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office (AARO) released its fiscal year 2024 annual report covering May 2023 to June 2024. Of 757 reports received, every resolved case turned out to be ordinary objects like balloons, birds, or drones, and AARO states it has found no evidence of extraterrestrial technology — though nearly 60% of cases remain unresolved due to insufficient data.

This is the annual report from AARO, the Pentagon office established to investigate unidentified anomalous phenomena (UAP). It covers reports received between May 2023 and June 2024 and was cleared for public release on 7 November 2024, with a formal release date of 5 February 2025. AARO received 757 UAP reports during this period: 485 from incidents within the reporting window, and 272 older cases from 2021–2022 that had not previously been submitted. Of those 757 reports, 392 came from a bulk transfer of all Federal Aviation Administration UAP reports since 2021. Of the cases AARO was able to resolve — 118 during the reporting period, plus 174 pending formal closure — all were identified as ordinary objects: balloons, birds, unmanned aerial systems (drones), satellites including the Starlink constellation, and conventional aircraft. None indicated advanced foreign technology or aerospace breakthroughs. AARO states explicitly that it has discovered no evidence of extraterrestrial beings, activity, or technology. However, 444 cases — 59% of the total — were placed in an "Active Archive" because they lacked sufficient data for analysis, and 170 reports (22.4%) contained too little information to even describe the shape of what was observed. Only 21 cases were flagged as meriting deeper analysis by intelligence community and science partners. Notably, AARO received no data from the United States' most capable sensor systems — its national signals intelligence (SIGINT), geospatial intelligence (GEOINT), and measurement and signature intelligence (MASINT) platforms contributed zero reports. The report highlights several specific incidents. Three reports described pilots being trailed or shadowed by UAP, though AARO has no confirmation these are linked to foreign adversaries. A commercial aircrew reported a near miss with a cylindrical object over the Atlantic Ocean off the coast of New York. Eighteen incidents involved drones near U.S. nuclear infrastructure, including a crashed drone recovered at the D.C. Cook Nuclear Power Plant in Michigan on 3 August 2023 and six consecutive nights of drone flyovers at a nuclear fuel facility in Lynchburg, Virginia, in October 2023. No adverse health effects were reported by any observers. On the technology front, AARO introduced GREMLIN, a prototype sensor system developed by the Georgia Tech Research Institute, which successfully collected data during a test in March 2024. The office also launched its public website, aaro.mil, on 31 October 2023, and coordinated with Oak Ridge National Laboratory on a materials analysis of an alleged UAP sample, with results posted publicly.

What to know

  • This is an official U.S. government report representing AARO's institutional position — it is not an independent scientific assessment, and a classified version may contain additional findings.
  • 59% of cases (444 of 757) were shelved in an Active Archive due to insufficient data, meaning the majority of sightings could not be analysed at all, which limits the strength of any conclusions about what UAP are or are not.
  • The 392 FAA reports were a bulk historical transfer of all FAA UAP data since 2021, which inflates the period's numbers and may distort trend analysis.
  • None of the United States' most capable intelligence sensor systems (GEOINT, SIGINT, MASINT) contributed any data to this analysis, meaning the report draws on less sophisticated sources.
  • All 49 reports classified as occurring in the space domain came from pilots or ground observers estimating altitude, not from space-based sensors, making those altitude classifications unreliable.
