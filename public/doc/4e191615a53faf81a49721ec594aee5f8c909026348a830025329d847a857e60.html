1948 Air Force Report Catalogues 100 Early UFO Sightings

Department of War 1948 Various ✓ verified against source

This is the initial progress report of Project Sign, the US Air Force's first official investigation into unidentified flying objects, activated on 26 January 1948 at Wright-Patterson Air Force Base. It tabulates and analyses 100 UFO sighting reports from 1947 and 1948, noting recurring features such as disc or saucer shapes, high climb rates and speeds ranging from hovering to supersonic. Notably, the report reaches no firm conclusion about what the objects were, and includes a reprinted magazine article suggesting the sightings could be explained by unconventional aircraft.

This document is the first progress report of Project Sign, an early US Air Force effort to investigate reports of unidentified flying objects (UFOs). The project — originally designated MX-304 — was activated on 26 January 1948 at Air Materiel Command (AMC), Wright-Patterson Air Force Base, in response to a US Air Force letter dated 30 December 1947 signed by General L. C. Craigie. A related Technical Instruction, numbered 2185 and dated 11 February 1948, was also published. The report was signed by Colonel H. M. McCoy, Chief of Intelligence. The core of the report is a tabulation and analysis of 100 UFO incidents reported through 1 February 1948, drawn from sightings across the United States and abroad during 1947 and 1948. It records recurring characteristics: objects were described as oval, disc or saucer-shaped in 51 of the cases; reported sizes ranged from that of a 25-cent coin to 250 feet in diameter, or from the size of a pursuit plane to the bulk of six B-29 aircraft. Most sightings (77) involved a single object. Exhaust trails were reported 23 times and an associated sound was noted 11 times. Estimated speeds spanned the full range from very slow or hovering to supersonic. The investigators sought outside expertise, consulting Dr. Irving Langmuir of the General Electric Research Laboratories in Schenectady, New York. Langmuir was reluctant to treat the so-called 'flying discs' as a reality and said the available data was insufficient for a positive identification. Investigators also cross-checked mundane explanations, such as the schedules of lighted night-flying advertising blimps. Rather than reaching a conclusion about the objects' origin, AMC advanced a hypothesis: it believed it possible to construct a 'low-aspect-ratio' aircraft — one with unusually short, wide wings — that could duplicate many of the reported appearances and behaviours through careful aerodynamic design. This is presented as a possible explanation, not a confirmed identification. The report also handles individual cases with scrutiny: photographs from one incident (Incident 40, reported by a Mr. Rhoades) were enlarged and evaluated at AMC, while another case (Incident 18, in Toronto) was determined to be a deliberate hoax staged for publicity and money. Attached to the report is a third-party magazine article, 'The Biology of the Flying Saucer' by A. R. Weyl, published in The Aeroplane on 13 February 1948, which argues along similar aerodynamic lines. The document was originally classified CONFIDENTIAL and later declassified under authority NND 927545. The version available is a scan of a 1948 typewritten original, and the underlying text has been read by optical character recognition with heavy transcription errors, so some names, numbers and dates in the source are garbled.

What to know

  • The report does not identify what the objects were. Its low-aspect-ratio-aircraft explanation is an explicit hypothesis, and the outside expert consulted (Dr. Langmuir) said the data was insufficient for any positive identification.
  • The 100 incidents are eyewitness accounts of varying reliability. The report itself flags one observer as prone to exaggeration and confirms one case as a deliberate hoax staged for publicity and money.
  • The dataset is incomplete: many fields in the tabulation are recorded as 'Not stated'.
  • The attached article, 'The Biology of the Flying Saucer', is an opinion piece from an aviation magazine, not an official Air Force finding.
  • The document is a scan of a 1948 typewritten original read by imperfect OCR, so specific names, figures and dates may contain transcription errors; redaction of content appears minimal beyond standard classification markings.
