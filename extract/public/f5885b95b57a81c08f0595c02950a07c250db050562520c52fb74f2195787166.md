# U.S. Navy Crew Tracked Fast Low-Flying Object Off Syria in 2016

**Department of War · 11/18/16 · Syria**

> A 2016 Department of War mission report describes a U.S. Navy P-8A patrol aircraft crew spotting an unidentified object flying low and fast — roughly 500 knots — over the Eastern Mediterranean near Latakia, Syria. The crew tracked it for about two minutes before losing sight, and the military's own assessment concluded it was likely routine Russian naval activity, not something unexplained.

On 18 November 2016, a U.S. Navy P-8A maritime patrol aircraft was operating in the Eastern Mediterranean when its electro-optical/infrared sensor picked up an unidentified object flying at very low altitude — what aviators call "sea skim mode" — at roughly 500 knots on a southeasterly heading. The aircraft was about 26 nautical miles south of the object when it was detected. The crew tracked the object for approximately two minutes before losing visual contact about 40 nautical miles northwest of Latakia, Syria.

During the brief observation, the object was seen passing between the Russian ship INGUL ARS and one unidentified vessel. The crew initially described what they saw as a "possible missile launch," and the report notes this was the first time a P-8 crew had observed possible missile activity in the Eastern Mediterranean. The mission commander characterized the encounter as safe, and visibility conditions were clear with no range limitations.

The military's own chain-of-command assessment concluded the activity was "standard activity consistent with" the Russian carrier task group (referred to as the KCTG) operating in the area at the time. In other words, the people responsible for evaluating the event leaned toward a conventional explanation — likely a Russian naval exercise — rather than treating it as truly anomalous.

The document was declassified by MG Richard A. Hamson, USCENTCOM Chief of Staff, and was approved for release to AARO (the Pentagon's UAP investigation office) on 27 March 2026 — nearly a decade after the original event. Video footage was recorded but the link to it is redacted, along with location coordinates and portions of the timeline. The label "UAP" appears in the document's title, but the body text consistently uses the phrase "possible missile," suggesting the UAP classification may have been applied later during the AARO review process rather than at the time of the event.

## What to know

- This is a single-source observation from one aircraft crew with only about two minutes of tracking time — there is no corroborating sensor data or second-platform confirmation in the document.
- Several sections are redacted, including exact coordinates, a link to the video footage, and parts of the event timeline, which limits independent verification.
- The military's own assessment at the time concluded this was routine Russian naval activity, not something unexplained — the "UAP" label in the title may have been added years later when the document was reviewed for the AARO program.
- The crew described the object as a "possible missile," which was their subjective assessment, not a confirmed identification of what the object actually was.
- The document was released to AARO in March 2026, nearly ten years after the event, meaning the context in which it was re-evaluated is very different from the context in which it was originally written.
