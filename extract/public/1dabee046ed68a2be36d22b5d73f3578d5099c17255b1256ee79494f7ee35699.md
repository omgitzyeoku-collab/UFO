# American Airlines Pilot Reported Multiple Moving Lights at 35,000 Feet

**FAA · 2023**

> This FAA SKYWATCH incident report documents an American Airlines pilot observing multiple lights moving in different directions near the constellation Leo while flying from Los Angeles to Dallas/Fort Worth at 35,000 feet on 9 October 2022. The case was closed with no explanation or resolution provided in the document.

This document is an FAA SKYWATCH incident report filed under the primary code "Unidentified Aerial Phenomenon." It records an observation made by the crew of American Airlines flight AAL2438, an Airbus A321 operating a commercial route from Los Angeles (LAX) to Dallas/Fort Worth (DFW).

On 9 October 2022, beginning at 09:55 UTC, the crew reported seeing multiple lights moving in different directions while flying eastbound at flight level 350 (35,000 feet) over a point identified as CNX. The lights were described as appearing to the left of the constellation Leo, which suggests the crew was looking upward or at a significant altitude above them. The observation was still continuing at the time the report was filed at 10:25 UTC. The report was routed through the Albuquerque Air Route Traffic Control Center (ZAB), and the incident triggered a page to relevant personnel.

The FAA marked this case as closed. No resolution, identification, or explanation for the lights is provided anywhere in the document. There is no mention of corroborating radar data, additional witness reports from other aircraft, or follow-up investigation.

No tail number for the aircraft was recorded in the report, and the location identifier CNX does not correspond to a well-known major US airport — it may refer to a navigational waypoint or VOR station along the route.

## What to know

- This is a single-source pilot observation with no corroborating sensor data, radar returns, or independent witness reports mentioned in the document.
- The reference to the constellation Leo raises the possibility that the lights were astronomical in nature or at very high altitude — the document does not address this.
- The case is marked closed with no explanation, meaning the FAA did not publicly resolve what the lights were based on this record alone.
- No tail number was recorded, which is unusual for an incident report and limits independent verification of the specific aircraft.
- The location identifier CNX is not a standard major airport code, making the precise location of the sighting harder to confirm without additional navigational data.
